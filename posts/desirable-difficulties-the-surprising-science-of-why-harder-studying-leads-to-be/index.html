Desirable Difficulties: The Surprising Science of Why Harder Studying Leads to Better Grades

2026-08-03 · Productivity · Tags: Desirable, productivity, studyhackspro

Imagine two students preparing for the same biology exam. One spends an hour re-reading her textbook, highlighting key passages, and reviewing her notes. The other spends that hour quizzing herself with flashcards, mixing topics, and trying to explain concepts from memory. The first student feels confident—the material seems familiar, almost easy. The second student struggles, often drawing blanks and feeling frustrated. Yet when the exam arrives, it's the second student who performs better. Why? Because the second student embraced what cognitive psychologists call desirable difficulties—challenges that make learning feel harder in the moment but dramatically improve long-term retention and understanding. In this article, we'll explore the science behind this counterintuitive principle and show you exactly how to harness it to transform your study sessions from comfortable but ineffective to challenging and powerful.

What Are Desirable Difficulties?

Coined by psychologist Robert Bjork in the 1990s, the term desirable difficulties refers to learning conditions that slow down acquisition and make practice more effortful, but which ultimately lead to stronger memory and better transfer of knowledge. These difficulties are "desirable" because they trigger deeper cognitive processing, forcing your brain to work harder to encode and retrieve information. The key is that the difficulty is not arbitrary—it's specifically designed to engage the mental processes that build durable learning.

Common examples include:

  • Spacing out study sessions instead of cramming
  • Interleaving different topics or types of problems rather than blocking them
  • Testing yourself instead of re-reading
  • Generating answers or explanations before seeing the correct response
  • Varying the context or conditions of practice

Each of these techniques makes studying feel slower and more error-prone. But that struggle is precisely what signals to your brain that this information matters—and it forces you to build connections, retrieve from memory, and apply knowledge in flexible ways. The result? You remember more, understand deeper, and perform better on exams.

The Science Behind the Struggle

Why do desirable difficulties work? The answer lies in how memory works. When you re-read a textbook, you're engaging in a process called fluency—the information feels easy because it's right in front of you. Your brain processes it passively, and you mistake familiarity for knowledge. But true learning requires active reconstruction. When you force yourself to recall information without cues, you strengthen the neural pathways that allow you to access that information later. This is known as the testing effect, and it's one of the most robust findings in cognitive psychology.

Similarly, spacing out your study sessions exploits the spacing effect. When you revisit material after a delay, your brain has to work to retrieve it, and that effortful retrieval consolidates the memory. Interleaving, meanwhile, forces you to discriminate between different concepts, which improves your ability to choose the right strategy for a given problem—a skill that's essential for exams.

Research by Bjork and others has shown that these difficulties lead to better long-term retention, even though they often produce lower performance during practice. In one classic study, students who studied with spaced practice forgot less over a week than those who crammed, even though the crammers performed better immediately after studying. The lesson is clear: if you want to remember material for weeks or months—not just until the next class—you need to make your studying harder.

Putting Desirable Difficulties into Practice

Now that you understand the theory, let's look at how to apply it to your own study routine. The key is to intentionally introduce difficulties that are challenging but not overwhelming. Here are four evidence-based strategies you can start using today.

1. Space Your Study Sessions – Instead of studying a subject for three hours on Sunday, break it into three one-hour sessions spread across the week. Use a spaced repetition app like Anki or a simple calendar to schedule reviews. The ideal gap is about 10-20% of the time you want to remember the material—so if you have an exam in a month, review every 3-4 days.

2. Interleave Related Topics – When you're studying math, don't do all the quadratic equations first and then all the linear ones. Mix them up. Similarly, when studying history, alternate between different eras or themes. This forces your brain to constantly switch gears and identify the underlying principles that distinguish each concept. It's harder, but it builds the kind of flexible knowledge that exam questions often require.

3. Test Yourself, Don't Just Re-read – Turn your notes into flashcards, or use practice questions from past exams. Cover the answers and try to recall them. Even better, try to explain a concept out loud as if you were teaching it to someone else. This is called self-explanation, and it's a powerful form of generation. If you get it wrong, that's okay—the error is a signal that your brain needs to strengthen that connection.

4. Vary Your Practice Conditions – Study in different locations, at different times of day, and with different tools (e.g., handwritten notes one day, digital flashcards the next). This variability makes the memory less context-dependent, so you can recall it in the exam room, which is a different environment from your desk.

Embracing the Discomfort: A Mindset Shift

The biggest obstacle to using desirable difficulties is psychological. When you study and it feels hard, you naturally assume you're not learning. But the opposite is true. You need to reframe that discomfort as a sign of growth. Think of it like a workout: if you lift weights and it's easy, you're not building muscle. The burn is what makes you stronger. The same applies to your brain.

To make this shift, start by setting expectations. Before each study session, remind yourself: "This will feel challenging, and that's a good thing." When you catch yourself feeling frustrated because you can't recall a fact, celebrate it—that's your brain working hard. You can also track your progress over time. Notice that after a few weeks of spaced practice, you're recalling information more easily than you would have with cramming. That evidence will reinforce the habit.

Another helpful technique is to use the Feynman Technique—explain a concept in simple terms as if teaching a child. If you struggle, you've found a gap in your understanding. That gap is a desirable difficulty; it tells you exactly what to review. Instead of avoiding the struggle, lean into it.

Avoiding the Pitfalls: When Difficulties Become Undesirable

Not all difficulties are desirable. If a task is so hard that you can't make any progress, you'll become discouraged and learn nothing. The key is to find the sweet spot—challenging but achievable. Here are some common pitfalls to avoid:

  • Over-spacing: If you wait too long between reviews, you'll forget too much and have to relearn from scratch. Use a schedule that keeps the material just on the edge of your memory.
  • Interleaving too many topics: If you switch between completely unrelated subjects (e.g., chemistry and poetry), you won't build the necessary connections. Interleave topics that are similar enough to require discrimination.
  • Testing without feedback: Self-testing is only effective if you check your answers and correct errors. Always review the correct response after attempting a recall.
  • Ignoring foundational knowledge: If you're a beginner, you need some initial exposure before you can benefit from retrieval practice. Start with a brief review, then test yourself.

To avoid these pitfalls, start small. Pick one technique—say, spacing—and implement it for a week. Then add another. Monitor your performance on practice quizzes. If you're consistently scoring below 60%, you might be making it too hard. Adjust the difficulty by shortening the spacing interval or reducing the number of interleaved topics.

Conclusion

The idea that learning should be easy is a myth. In reality, the most effective study methods are often the ones that feel the most difficult. By embracing desirable difficulties—spacing, interleaving, testing, and generation—you can transform your study sessions from passive re-reading into active, challenging, and deeply effective practice. Yes, it will feel uncomfortable at first. You'll make mistakes, you'll struggle, and you might even doubt yourself. But that struggle is the very process that builds lasting knowledge. So the next time you sit down to study, don't reach for the highlighter. Reach for a blank sheet of paper, a set of flashcards, and a willingness to be challenged. Your grades—and your brain—will thank you.
